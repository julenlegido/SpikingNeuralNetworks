**Towards energy-efficient brain-inspired AI: An overview and evaluation of Spiking Neural Networks**

Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) have emerged as a promising alternative to conventional Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) due to their biologically inspired computation and potential for energy-efficient processing. Unlike traditional neural networks, SNNs communicate through discrete spike events distributed over time, enabling sparse and event-driven information processing.

This thesis investigates the behavior of SNNs in comparison with ANN models across both static and event-based image classification tasks. Experiments were conducted using MNIST, CIFAR-10, N-MNIST, and CIFAR10-DVS datasets, evaluating not only classification accuracy but also spike activity metrics, temporal dynamics, and computational efficiency. Several encoding strategies, architectural modifications, and optimization techniques were explored, including rate encoding, gradient-based encoding, normalization methods, and temporal parameter tuning.

The results show that SNNs can achieve competitive performance, particularly on datasets containing meaningful temporal information. However, challenges related to input encoding, optimization stability and hardware execution remain significant. A theoretical operation-count analysis further suggests that the computational advantages of SNNs become most evident when sparse spike activity is executed on dedicated neuromorphic hardware. Overall, the work highlights both the current limitations and future potential of spike-based neural computation.
